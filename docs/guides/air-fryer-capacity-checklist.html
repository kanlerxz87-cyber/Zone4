Which air fryer capacity do you need? A no-hype checklist

Capacity is the most quoted number on a listing and the least consistent one. Compare it in one unit, check the title against the spec, and ignore the rest of the hype.

Read the unit before the number

The three listings do not state capacity the same way. P3's title says 8 Qt [F7] and its recorded capacity is 8 quarts [F8]. P2's title says 26QT [F3], while its recorded capacity is 24.6 liters [F9]. P1's title says 6 Quart [F10], but its recorded capacity is 6 liters [F11].

The P1 pair is not a rounding difference we can settle: six quarts and six litres are different volumes. We have not converted or chosen between them. It is recorded as a flag, so you can check the official listing yourself.

What a capacity figure does not tell you

A capacity figure is a total, not a usable area. Two-basket models split it, and a deep basket is not the same as a wide one. Use the dimensions alongside the capacity, and the cooking area the listing describes, before deciding that a larger number means more food.

Judge capacity against the meals you cook: how many portions, and whether they are one large item (a whole chicken) or several small ones (fries for four).

The checklist

1. Note the unit the listing uses (quarts, qt or liters) and convert every listing to that unit yourself before comparing.

2. Check the title figure against the spec figure. If they differ, treat the spec as unconfirmed until you have checked the page.

3. Decide whether you need one basket or two, using the meal sizes you actually cook.

4. Ignore claims of a single 'best' capacity: none of the listings states a test of cooking results, and this site does not run one.
